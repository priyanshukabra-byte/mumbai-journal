Day One · Friday, 21 August

The City Introduces Itself

Kalyan → Dombivli → Thane → South Bombay → Powai

I landed in the morning with nothing planned but a friend's address in Kalyan. By evening I'd ridden a stranger's bus, argued my way through a packed local, and lost a miniature F1 car to the crowd. Mumbai doesn't wait for you to catch up. It just carries you along.

It started small: a bike ride to a canal and garden corner of Casa Elite society, bun maska and cutting chai at some early hour, and a nap that stretched to 2pm. Then the day tilted upright. An auto to Dombivli station, my first ever Mumbai local, and against every statistic, a seat. A stop in Thane for burgers at Jimi's. The Aqua Line metro carried us an hour into South Bombay, and stepping out felt like walking into a film I'd already seen a hundred times without knowing it.

We walked toward BSE, past Bombay House, past Kala Ghoda's black horse, until the Gateway of India rose out of the grey monsoon sky exactly like it does in every postcard, except this one smelled like the sea. Twenty thousand steps later I'd talked a street vendor down to a fair price for two miniature cars, an F1 and a Mustang. Souvenirs, I told myself. I didn't know yet that one of them wouldn't make it home.

Late for the rented bike waiting in Dombivli, we caught a red BMC bus. Conductors here are called "master." A driver's emergency brake nearly threw me off my seat, which became the funniest near miss of the trip. Then came CST, glowing under strings of yellow lamps, filling with the 7pm crowd like water finding its level. Walking in felt like walking into Hogwarts. Getting onto the return local felt like walking into a scrum: by the third stop there was no air left, only bodies. A shove, a shouted argument in Marathi my friend handled and I didn't understand a word of, and suddenly we were spat out onto the platform, one miniature Mustang short of the pair. The F1 car never made it out of that crowd.

We got the bike at 8:45, ten minutes past the mark, and remembered we'd promised two friends we'd meet them at a club called Rude, in Powai, at 9. Google Maps said ninety minutes. We had jeans, one helmet each, and a fair amount of denial. Somewhere on the expressway at 160 km/h, mid-attempt at tying a handkerchief over my face against the dust, my glasses slid off my nose and were gone. A quiet, almost polite kind of loss, compared to what the night had already taken.

We reached at 11. Danced far harder than the lateness deserved. Got properly, gloriously drunk, drunk enough that getting home felt scarier than the night itself. Somewhere near 2am I was sitting on a curb outside the club with no memory of how I got there. Dipanjan rode us back, stopping twice because I kept drifting off on the pillion seat. I only found out about the stops the next morning. That was day one. Mumbai took a pair of glasses and half a souvenir set, and gave me a story I still can't tell without grinning.

Day Two · Saturday, 22 August

Malshej Ghat, on Two Wheels

Kalyan → Malshej Ghat → Kalyan, ~260km round trip

The hangover arrived before I opened my eyes. It left somewhere around the fortieth kilometre of a 411cc bike doing 120 through a monsoon-green ghat, rain in my face, songs in my ears, and no plan beyond the front wheel.

The debate was Lonavala or Malshej Ghat. Malshej won. We geared up in full riding kit for a walk to a South Indian place inside the society, helmets and jackets over a stomach that wasn't sure about food yet, and ordered uttapam and dosa with filter coffee at the very reasonable hour of noon. Then a cold engine start, a rented Hero XPulse and a Royal Enfield Scram 411, and the first proper motorcycle ride of my life.

The first stretch was slow city traffic, then the ghat simply opened. Waterfalls fell across the road itself; people used them to rinse their bikes mid-ride. Clouds sat low enough to stand inside. Somewhere past the 40km mark the mist thickened, the hills rose, and cold wind found every gap in my jacket while a playlist ran in my headset. The kind of thing that doesn't need explaining, only more road.

The return leg was every bit as good as the way in, this time in proper monsoon downpour, standing on the pegs through water crossings, visor fogged, machine steady underneath. Then the city traffic hit and the smoothness of the ghat disappeared into gridlock. We dropped the bike at 8:30 sharp, mileage almost mythically exact, minus one bent rear indicator we settled with the owner on the spot. He took it with more grace than the moment deserved.

Dinner had to be phoned in, a restaurant not on Zomato or Swiggy, phone orders only, eaten sitting on the floor of the hall. Then the day's real crisis surfaced: Dipanjan's phone, soaked on the ride, wouldn't turn on. We sat with that helplessness a while, then did what two tired boys do when the future is uncertain and the wifi still works: shared a tablet and watched reels until sleep took over. I run my house with Dettol and disinfectant every single day. His flat was the exact opposite. For one weekend, that didn't matter at all.

Day Three · Sunday, 23 August

GRRR Underground & Seedhe Maut

Kalyan → Byculla — drift cars, a well of death, and Imran Khan

We planned to arrive by 2pm. We arrived at 4. In between: a shady bar, a manufacturing shed turned drifting arena, a live MMA fight, a maut ka kua I got into as the 21st and final person allowed, standing two feet from Imran Khan, and a stage that said Seedhe Maut in letters made of red light.

We woke at 11:30 knowing the ride to Byculla would eat over an hour, and behaved with the calm of people who had nowhere to be. By the time we were dressed, fed on last night's leftovers, and on the road, the traffic had its own plans: an hour and a half in heavy rain with no raincoat, just an upper jacket, riding pillion with a sore back from yesterday's 260km. Parking was its own small war. But Dipanjan's advice held: don't get tired so early, we have a long day ahead.

Inside the venue, a genuinely dead-looking manufacturing shed brought back to life, the first thing that hit was the fashion. Then the scale: human football arenas sponsored by MuscleBlaze, a Monster Energy stall, Royal Enfield showing off the new Guerrilla up front, a live MMA fight mid ring, and further in, cars drifting and bikes doing stunts close enough to feel the heat. I'd never watched any of it live before.

The Maut Ka Kua queue ran half an hour, and just as we reached the front, Imran Khan walked into the very same area with his crew. The guard capped entry at 21 people, and I was the 21st. I watched the Well of Death standing a few feet from an actor I'd grown up watching, got a photo and a few words with him, and came back down still not quite believing it. Then the crowd surged for Seedhe Maut, sweating, singing, jumping, tired in the good way, and by pure timing we stepped out just as event security sealed the gate behind us for VIP exit. Their car pulled up right beside us on the way out.

We chased one more thing before the day ended: Marine Drive, because visiting Mumbai without seeing it felt unfinished. Google Maps had one final trick, routing us straight through a red light district, phones pocketed, trying to laugh instead of panic. We got out fast, and I promised myself, mostly seriously, to never trust that app blindly again. Marine Drive itself, close to eleven at night, was everything the detour wasn't: quiet, lit, full of couples and music and people just sitting by the sea. Parked bike, a hundred rupees for parking, half an hour of doing nothing, then pav bhaji eaten sitting on the street, the most Mumbai meal of the whole trip. Home after another hour long ride, and finally, sleep.

Day Four · Monday, 24 August

Leaving, Slowly

The original plan had me leaving Sunday night, in time for Monday's office. Keeping that plan would have meant missing everything that actually happened. So I changed it, because life is for making memories, not running like a machine that clocks in on schedule.

Dipanjan left for office; I let the morning move slowly, lunch, a movie, rest. In the evening, puchka and vada pav, one last taste of the street before the cab arrived. Growing up, everyone around me warned me off this city, too crowded, too much, not for me. I believed it too, once. What I found instead is that a city is only its people, not the other way around. Mumbai took a pair of glasses and half a souvenir set. It gave me a lifetime of memory in exchange.

Thank you, Mumbai — for everything.
